Why 2025-26 did not move, and why 2026-27 is different

A determination is not always written one year at a time, and the 2 most recent ones are drafted differently. The 2024 determination set the rate 'for income years commencing on or after 1 July 2024', and its own note says it applies to the year commencing 1 July 2024 and to any subsequent income year until it is repealed or varied. Nothing replaced it for 2025-26, so 88 cents carried through unchanged.

The 2026 determination repealed it and is written for one year only. Section 6 sets the rate 'for the income year commencing on 1 July 2026', and it carries no note extending that to subsequent years. So 91 cents does not carry forward the way 88 cents did. A further determination has to be registered to set the rate for 2027-28, and until one is registered, there is no rate for that year to rely on.

How the method works

For business claims, the ATO limits this method to sole traders and partnerships with at least one individual partner, claiming for a car. Private travel does not qualify. This eligibility guidance was checked on 22 September 2026; the historical rate table review remains dated 14 September 2026.

Section 28-25 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1997 sets the calculation: business kilometres travelled by the car in the income year, multiplied by the rate determined for that year. Four features of it catch people out.

  • 5,000 kilometres is a ceiling, not a threshold. If the car travelled 5,085 business kilometres, you claim 5,000 and lose the extra 85. The Act uses that exact example.
  • One rate for every car. Engine size stopped mattering when the old engine capacity bands were replaced by a single determined rate.
  • No substantiation of the car expenses. Section 28-35 removes that requirement for this method, which is not the same as needing no records: the business kilometres still have to be a reasonable estimate you can explain.
  • The rate is meant to cover everything. In setting it, the Commissioner must have regard to average operating costs, which the Act’s own note describes as including registration, insurance and depreciation as well as fuel and maintenance. Claiming those separately on top of the rate is double counting.

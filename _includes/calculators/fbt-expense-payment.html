Sources applied

  • Valuation: sections 20, 22A and 23 of the Fringe Benefits Tax Assessment Act 1986 (Compilation No. 97, C2026C00332, 1 July 2026), read 19 November 2026. Section 20 makes paying or reimbursing an employee’s expenditure a benefit. Section 23 values an external expense payment at the payment, less the employee’s contribution, or the reimbursement. Section 22A values an in-house property or residual expense payment as if the goods or service were an in-house benefit under section 42, 48 or 49, the recipient’s contribution being the expenditure less the payment net of the contribution, or less the reimbursement.
  • Otherwise deductible rule: section 24(1) takes the notional deduction, GD − RD, off the taxable value, TV − ND. For an external expense payment GD is the once-only deduction the employee would have had for the whole expenditure, unreimbursed; RD is the deduction for the part they bore, the recipient’s portion in subsection 136(1). Section 9.4 of ATO: Fringe benefits tax - a guide for employers, chapter 9, read 19 November 2026, sets out the same steps for both ways a reimbursement can be set.
  • In-house reduction: section 62(1) reduces the total taxable value of an employee’s in-house fringe benefits for the year by up to $1,000; section 62(2) excludes a benefit provided under a salary packaging arrangement.
  • Examples: section 9.3 of the guide: petroleum a retailer bought from the employer for $800 and sold to an employee for $1,000, with $250 reimbursed, valued as the retailer’s sale to the employee, the lesser of its $800 cost and the $1,000 notional value: $800 − ($1,000 − $250) = $50; section 9.4: $500 spent, 80% for work, $250 reimbursed without regard to the business share ($50) and $350 reimbursed for it (nil).
  • Rounding: the gross deduction and 75% of a price or value each round half up to the cent; the Act prescribes no rounding. Supplied by you: the kind of payment, the expenditure, the payment or reimbursement, the contribution, the income use share and the deduction for the part the employee bore, or the in-house rule’s price or notional value and the in-house reduction left.

How the FBT expense payment calculator works

The FBT expense payment calculator values one expense payment fringe benefit in the FBT year from 1 April 2026 to 31 March 2027: your payment of an employee’s expenditure to a third party, or your reimbursement of it. An external expense payment is valued at the payment, less any contribution the employee made towards it, or at the reimbursement. Where the employee could have deducted the expenditure at once, the otherwise deductible rule reduces the value: the gross deduction is the expenditure × the share that would be deductible; the notional deduction is that less the deduction for the part the employee bore. The taxable value is the payment less the contribution and the notional deduction, never below nil.

Where the employee bought goods or a service that you, or an associate, sell to customers in the ordinary course of business, the payment is an in-house expense payment. The calculator values the goods or service by the in-house rule that applies to the seller, as if the seller’s sale to the employee were an in-house benefit (section 22A), and takes off the part of the expenditure the employee bore, the expenditure less your payment net of their contribution, then the part of the employee’s $1,000 in-house reduction for the year that their other in-house benefits have not used, outside salary packaging.

This page opens on the first example in section 9.4 of the ATO’s guide for employers: an employee spends $500, 80% for work, and you reimburse $250 without regard to the business share. The gross deduction of $400 less the employee’s deduction of ($500 − $250) × 80% = $200 leaves a notional deduction of $200, so the taxable value is $250 − $200 = $50. The tests also hold the guide’s second section 9.4 example and its section 9.3 in-house example.

What the FBT expense payment calculator does not cover

The calculator does not decide whether an expense payment fringe benefit arises or whether an exemption applies, such as a no-private-use declaration (section 20A), accommodation away from home (section 21), car expenses (section 22) or the exemptions in Division 13. It does not apply the otherwise deductible rule to an in-house expense payment (subparagraph 24(1)(b)(i)): choose 0% and $0 for one. It does not value airline transport, a car expense, whose otherwise deductible amount follows the car substantiation rules, an expense paid for an employee and an associate jointly, whose reduction is limited to the employee’s interest, the reduction for entertainment of others in section 63A, the living-away-from-home food reduction in section 63, or several payments for the same expenditure (subsection 22A(4)). It does not check the documentary evidence, declaration or travel diary the otherwise deductible rule needs (paragraphs 24(1)(c) to (e)) or work out the employee’s deduction. It values one benefit: the $1,000 in-house reduction is for all of an employee’s in-house fringe benefits in the year, so enter what the others have not used. Goods or services you buy and give an employee, or buy on your credit card, are property or residual benefits, not expense payments. It stops at the taxable value and does not gross it up or work out FBT. Confirm the figures with the ATO’s guide or a registered tax agent.

Sources applied

  • Car: a motor vehicle, except a motor cycle or similar vehicle, designed to carry a load of less than 1 tonne and fewer than 9 passengers, from section 995-1 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1997, which the GST Act (section 195-1) and the FBT Act (subsection 136(1)) adopt. The ATO’s eligible vehicles page counts the driver among the passengers. The load is the gross vehicle mass on the compliance plate less the basic kerb weight, from paragraph 11 of MT 2024 and section 7.2.1 of the ATO’s FBT guide for employers, chapter 7, which adds that accessories fitted after manufacture do not change it. All read 16 November 2026.
  • Designed principally to carry passengers: a dual cab is when its seats, the driver’s included, at 68 kg each weigh more than the load left, from paragraphs 14 to 16 of MT 2024 (consolidated 22 October 2008; only its appendices are withdrawn). A passenger body is, as in the example in TD 94/19, which leaves any other body, a single cab ute or panel van included, to its appearance, marketing and specifications. TD 2006/39 applies the FBT design reasoning to section 40-230’s ‘designed mainly for carrying passengers’, and this page applies MT 2024’s dual cab method there on that footing. No ATO source says whether a single cab ute or panel van is designed mainly for carrying passengers, so the page leaves the car limit undecided for them. For FBT, paragraph 8(2)(a)(i) covers a panel van or utility truck without asking, and the eligible vehicles page lists a single cab ute and a panel or goods van.
  • Car limit: the first element of cost of a car designed mainly for carrying passengers, after any GST credit, is reduced to the car limit for the year you start to hold it, from section 40-230 of the Act and TD 2006/40. The limit is $69,883 for 2026-27 and $69,674 for 2025-26, from the car limit table.
  • GST credit: the GST on a car up to 1/11 of the car limit when its GST-inclusive market value exceeds the limit, from section 69-10 of the GST Act, read 16 November 2026. Subsection 69-10(4) lifts the cap for a car that subsection 25-1(2) of the LCT Act keeps from being a luxury car, and for a lease or hire.
  • Luxury car tax: a car whose value exceeds the threshold, unless it is a commercial vehicle not designed principally to carry passengers (paragraph 25-1(2)(c)); the fuel-efficient threshold applies at a combined rating of 3.5 litres per 100 km or less (subsection 25-1(4)); and an LCT car carries a load under 2 tonnes (section 27-1), all from the A New Tax System (Luxury Car Tax) Act 1999 (Compilation No. 27, 1 April 2025). The thresholds are from the ATO’s LCT rate and thresholds (last updated 1 June 2026): $91,661 and $80,809 for 2026-27, $91,387 and $80,567 for 2025-26. Under the compliance approach in paragraphs 50 to 58 of LCTD 2023/1, the ATO considers a passenger body unlikely to be a commercial vehicle, and will not review a cargo or delivery van, or a ute whose seats at 68 kg are less than half its load; paragraph 59 says that method does not decide a vehicle’s principal purpose. All read 16 November 2026.
  • FBT: a car benefit is exempt for a panel van, utility truck or other vehicle under 1 tonne not designed principally to carry passengers (subsection 8(2)), and a residual benefit for a motor vehicle that is not a car (subsection 47(6)), in each case only if private use was limited to work-related travel and other use that was minor, infrequent and irregular, from the Fringe Benefits Tax Assessment Act 1986, read 16 November 2026. PCG 2018/3 sets out when the ATO will not review that private use.
  • Records: Division 28 of the Act applies to an individual or a partnership with an individual partner (section 28-10); the log book method needs a log book of at least 12 continuous weeks, kept every fifth year, and odometer records (sections 28-100, 28-115 and 28-120); a vehicle that is not a car is claimed at actual cost from receipts, from the ATO’s motor vehicle expenses guide (last updated 29 May 2026), read 16 November 2026.
  • MT 2024, TD 94/19, TD 2006/39, TD 2006/40, LCTD 2023/1, PCG 2018/3 and the ATO’s guides are the Commissioner’s views, not the law itself.
  • Supplied by you: the specification, whether the vehicle was modified, the year, the price, the GST credit, the fuel consumption and the private use. The page does not test any of them.

How the vehicle classifier works

The vehicle classifier works out whether one vehicle bought in 2025-26 or 2026-27 is a car for income tax, GST and FBT, and what follows. The designed load is the gross vehicle mass on the compliance plate less the basic kerb weight, or the stated payload, which must agree with them. Under section 995-1 of ITAA 1997, a motor vehicle other than a motor cycle is a car if designed to carry a load under 1 tonne and fewer than 9 passengers, the driver counted.

For a car, the classifier asks whether it was designed principally to carry passengers. A passenger body was. A dual cab was if its seats, at 68 kg each (MT 2024), weigh more than the load left. For a single cab ute or panel van the answer turns on factors the page does not weigh (TD 94/19), so it leaves the car limit undecided. Then section 40-230 caps the cost to depreciate at the car limit, after any GST credit, and section 69-10 of the GST Act caps the credit at 1/11 of the limit.

Luxury car tax has its own car: a load under 2 tonnes and fewer than 9 passengers. The tax can apply above the threshold for its fuel consumption, unless it is a commercial vehicle not designed principally to carry passengers. For utes the classifier uses LCTD 2023/1’s simplified method, the ATO’s compliance approach: seats at 68 kg weighing less than half the load. The same answer decides the GST credit cap, which does not reach a car the LCT Act keeps from being a luxury car. At exactly half, the 2 tests part: MT 2024 counts a dual cab as designed for passengers only when its seats weigh more than the load left, whilst LCTD 2023/1’s method needs them to weigh less than half the load. A 5-seat dual cab with a 680 kg load can therefore be exempt from FBT, whilst luxury car tax and the GST credit cap are not decided here.

For FBT, a single cab ute, a panel van, a dual cab not designed principally for passengers and any vehicle that is not a car can be exempt only if an employee’s private use was work-related travel or minor, infrequent and irregular use. You state that fact; the classifier never concludes it. A car designed principally for passengers gives a car fringe benefit, which the FBT car calculator values.

TD 94/19’s 4-wheel drive wagon, with 5 seats and an 865 kg load, carries passengers; at $75,000 in 2025-26 the ATO’s instant asset write-off page gives a $6,334 credit and a $68,666 cost, or the $69,674 car limit without a credit. MT 2024’s dual cab has 340 kg of passengers against 260 kg left. LCTD 2023/1’s example 5, a dual cab with 5 seats and 960 kg, is a commercial vehicle. TD 2006/40’s $5,182 credit is $5,182.64 here, with its cents.

What the vehicle classifier does not cover

The classifier does not decide whether private use was minor, infrequent and irregular, or whether a single cab ute or panel van was designed mainly for carrying passengers. Nor does it decide whether a modification permanently changed a vehicle’s design (MT 2033 and LCTD 2023/1 paragraphs 16 to 30), or the principal purpose of a truck, bus or other body under 1 tonne, which turns on factors it does not weigh. Where LCTD 2023/1’s simplified method is not met it says the question is not decided here, and that method assumes a ute the Australian Design Rules class as a goods vehicle. It does not cover cars fitted out to carry people in wheelchairs, emergency vehicles, motor homes and campervans, taxis and limousines, leases and hire, a dealer quoting an ABN, a partly creditable purchase, or a year before 2025-26. It does not work out decline in value, luxury car tax payable or the taxable value of a fringe benefit. A classification is not advice: confirm it with the ATO’s guidance or a registered tax agent.

How the sole trader against company calculator works

As a sole trader the owner is taxed on the whole profit at the resident rates, less the low income tax offsets and the small business income tax offset, plus the Medicare levy. Drawings are not deductible, so the amount taken out changes nothing. Through a company the profit is split: the company pays the salary you enter and the super guarantee on it, both deductible, and pays company tax on the rest. The amount taken out less the salary is paid as a fully franked dividend from the profit after company tax, and whatever is left stays in the company. The owner pays tax and the Medicare levy on the salary (less the $1,000 standard deduction from 2026-27), the dividend and its franking credit, less the credit: the top-up tax is that tax less the tax on the salary alone, and is a refund where the credit is larger. The super fund pays 15% on the super guarantee, and Division 293 tax where taxable income and the contributions pass $250,000.

Each structure’s total adds those amounts for the one year. The 2 totals do not leave the money in the same place: the sole trader holds all the profit after tax, whilst the company structure leaves some in super and any profit not taken out in the company, which pays top-up tax when it is paid out as a dividend later. The calculator states each figure and the difference; it does not say which structure suits a business.

In the example, a $150,000 profit is taxed $38,570.00 as a sole trader, after the full $1,000 offset. Through a base rate entity paying a $60,000 salary and a $40,000 dividend, the total is $34,913.23, with $22,100.00 kept in the company and $6,120.00 in super.

What the sole trader against company calculator does not cover

The calculator does not apply the personal services income rules: where the profit is mainly a reward for the owner’s personal efforts, Part 2-42 can tax it to the owner whatever the company pays (see the PSI rules checker). It does not apply Division 7A: a payment, loan or debt forgiveness to the owner that is not salary or a declared dividend can be an unfranked deemed dividend (see the Division 7A calculator). It does not cover the small business CGT concessions or the CGT discount, which a company cannot claim; state taxes such as payroll tax, land tax and duty; or the cost of running a company, such as ASIC fees, its own tax return and its accounts.

It also leaves out the tax on profit kept in the company once it is paid out, the company’s franking account and a year in which the franking rate, set from last year’s figures, differs from the tax rate; other income of the owner, a spouse or family members, trusts and partnerships; GST, PAYG instalments and losses; the Medicare levy surcharge, study loan repayments and the low income super tax offset; a non-resident owner; and income years before 2021-22. Super counts at its value after tax, though it stays in the fund until retirement. Have a registered tax agent review any change of structure before relying on a result.

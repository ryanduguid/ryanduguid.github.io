## How the CGT concessions checker works

The CGT concessions checker takes one capital gain from selling an asset used in a business and works through Division 152 in the order the Act sets. It first tests the basic conditions in subsection 152-10(1): at least one of a CGT small business entity, the maximum net asset value test or a passively held asset, and the active asset test. Each of the 4 concessions then has its own conditions, and the result says, for each, whether it is open and which provision decides it.

A CGT small business entity carries on a business, or is winding one up, and has an aggregated turnover under $2 million: for the previous year, as likely for the year of the sale at its first day or the day the business started (unless both of the 2 years before were $2 million or more), or for the year of the sale at its end. For a partnership asset, the partnership is tested, not the partner. The net asset value test counts the market value of the CGT assets of you, your affiliates and the entities connected with you, less the liabilities and provisions related to them; an individual leaves out personal use assets, the main residence (except the share subsection 152-20(2A) counts for its use to produce income), super and life insurance (section 152-20).

The active asset test counts the days from the purchase contract to the sale contract, or to an earlier end of the business when the sale came within 12 months of it, both days included. An asset owned 15 years or less must have been active for at least half of those days; one owned more than 15 years, for at least 7.5 years. Because 7.5 years is 2,736 to 2,746 days, depending on the leap days and months the periods span, a total from 2,736 to 2,745 is refused: count it in years and months.

The 15-year exemption needs 15 years of continuous ownership before the sale and an individual aged 55 or over who is retiring, or one permanently incapacitated; for a company or trust, the individual is its significant individual, and it must have had one for a total of 15 years. When it applies, the whole gain is disregarded and the other concessions are not needed. Otherwise the 50% reduction applies automatically, and the retirement exemption and roll-over can follow in either order.

## What the CGT concessions checker does not cover

The checker says which concessions are open, and gives no advice. It does not work out the capital gain or the amount each concession takes off it. It does not cover:

- sale contracts from 1 July 2027, when Schedule 1 to the Treasury Laws Amendment (Tax Reform No. 1) Act 2026 changes Division 152, and before 1 July 2020;
- a share in a company or an interest in a trust, which must also meet the look-through conditions in subsection 152-10(2);
- a depreciating asset, whose gain is disregarded (section 118-24) or comes from CGT event K7, which section 152-10(1) excludes;
- CGT events other than a sale, including CGT events D1, J2, J5 and J6, and rights to partnership income or capital (subsection 152-10(2C));
- working out aggregated turnover, net value, affiliates, connected entities and small business participation percentages, which it takes from you;
- involuntary disposals and relationship breakdown roll-overs, which can extend the ownership and active periods (sections 152-45 and 152-115);
- public entities, which cannot choose the retirement exemption (subsection 152-305(3)), look-through earnout rights, and substituted accounting periods.

## How the PSI rules checker works

The PSI rules checker takes one individual’s personal services income for one income year and works through Part 2-42 in the order the Act sets. Each step on the result names the section it applies and what it found.

Income that is not mainly a reward for the individual’s personal efforts or skills is not PSI (subsection 84-5(1)), and income received as an employee or office holder falls outside Division 85 (subsection 85-35(1)). So does a payment to a religious practitioner by an entity carrying on an enterprise, for work in their religious vocation done as a member of a religious institution (subsection 85-35(2) and section 12-47 of Schedule 1 to the Taxation Administration Act 1953); check any other PSI they earn as a contractor. A personal services business determination in force settles the question (subsection 87-15(1)). Otherwise the results test comes first: if at least 75% of the PSI is paid for a result, with the plant and tools supplied and liability for defects, the PSI rules do not apply, however much comes from one payer (section 87-18).

If 80% or more of the PSI comes from one payer and its associates, the other 3 tests cannot be self-assessed and only a determination keeps the rules away (subsection 87-15(3)). The checker still works them out, because the Commissioner weighs them for a determination: 2 or more unrelated payers that engaged the individual or entity through offers to the public (section 87-20), principal work by others of at least 20% by market value or an apprentice for half the year (section 87-25), and qualifying business premises all year (section 87-30).

The checker reproduces the conclusion of each worked example in TR 2022/3 that reaches one on a test it applies, and of the matching examples on the ATO’s PSI pages.

## How to enter payers in the PSI rules checker

Enter each payer once, with its associates’ payments added in: the payer is whoever must pay under the contract, so a labour hire firm or agency is the payer, not its client (TR 2022/3 paragraphs 69 to 72). Payers that are associates of each other count as one payer too, as section 87-20 counts only payers that are not associates of each other: put them in one row, or choose the first of their rows under ‘Same payer as’ in each later one. Linked rows count as one payer in the unrelated clients test too, unless you give every payer with PSI a client label: rows with one label are one entity or associates of each other and rows with different labels are not, so 2 payers that are each an associate of a third, but not of each other, can count as 2 clients (paragraph 87-20(1)(a)). One label stays within one payer’s rows. Without labels, where 2 or more linked rows won by public offers would decide the outcome, the checker asks for labels. Count each Australian government agency as its own payer (section 87-35) and leave out income received as an employee. If the individual works through their own company, partnership or trust, choose that entity as who receives the income, even if it pays them a salary. Tick ‘Paid for a result’, ‘Supplies plant and tools’ and ‘Liable for defects’ only where the contract or industry custom makes it so (subsection 87-18(4)). The results test counts income, not payers, and a tick covers all of that row’s PSI: where only part of a payer’s PSI meets all 3, enter that part in a row of its own and choose the payer’s first row under ‘Same payer as’, so the 80% rule and section 87-20 still count one payer. For the market value of principal work by others, count work by any individual or entity engaged, an individual associate included, but not by an associate that is a company, partnership or trust, nor, for an entity, by an individual whose PSI it receives; a partner counts only if they earn no PSI through the partnership (subsections 87-25(1), (2) and (2A); TR 2022/3 paragraphs 110 to 116). With more than 5 payers, put the smallest together in one row only where every answer is the same; that can only make a test harder to pass.

## What the PSI rules checker leaves to you

The checker does not decide whether income is mainly a reward for personal efforts or skills, whether a contract pays for a result, whether an offer to the public won the work, who is an associate or what principal work is worth. These turn on facts and judgement, so it takes your answers and says so on each step; TR 2022/3 sets out the factors at paragraphs 34 to 49 and 77 to 148.

The checker does not allocate expenses across PSI, business income and other income. Division 85 limits deductions to the extent they relate to PSI (subsection 85-10(1)) but sets no method for the split, and TR 2022/3 leaves deductions to other rulings (paragraphs 6 and 31), so any split is practice rather than law. It does not work out the amount attributed under Division 86, PAYG withholding or a deduction for a net PSI loss.

The checker does not apply the agent rules when they could apply (section 87-40), decide a determination, which is the Commissioner’s (sections 87-60 and 87-65), or test Part IVA, which can still apply to a personal services business (TR 2022/3 paragraph 160). Several individuals working through one entity are tested one at a time (paragraph 76). Confirm the result with the ruling or a registered tax agent.

## How the casual and overtime pay calculator works

The calculator works one employee’s gross pay for one pay period from the rates you enter, then the super guarantee on the part that is ordinary time earnings. Ordinary hours are paid at the base hourly rate, plus the casual loading as a percentage of it for a casual. Each penalty or overtime line is its hours at the percentage of the base rate you enter, such as 150% for time and a half. Each hour is entered once, as an ordinary hour or on one line. Annual leave is paid at the base rate with any leave loading. Each amount is worked exactly and rounded to the cent once.

Ordinary time earnings are the pay for ordinary hours, the casual loading, penalty rates and shift loadings on ordinary hours, paid leave and a leave loading, unless the whole loading is paid for overtime the employee would have worked; a loading paid only partly for that is counted in full. Pay for overtime hours is not ordinary time earnings. For a payday from 1 July 2026, the super guarantee is 12% of qualifying earnings, which here are the ordinary time earnings in the pay; for a payday from 1 July 2025 to 30 June 2026, it is 12% of the ordinary time earnings under the quarterly rules. The calculator does not work out when the fund must receive the contribution: the SGC and Payday Super calculator does. The calculator refuses a pay whose ordinary time earnings alone exceed the maximum contribution base.

## What the casual and overtime pay calculator does not cover

The calculator applies no award, enterprise agreement or minimum rate, and does not check that the rates you enter meet one: the Fair Work Ombudsman’s pay guides and Pay and Conditions Tool give award rates. It does not decide which hours are ordinary hours or overtime, whether a casual loading compounds with a penalty rate, or how an award rounds its rates; enter those as your award or agreement states them. It does not check whether the employee or the earnings are excluded from the super guarantee. It leaves out allowances, commission, bonuses, salary sacrifice, other qualifying earnings, PAYG withholding, payroll tax and workers compensation, and it does not track the maximum contribution base across a quarter or year: it assumes earlier pays have not reached it. Paydays before 1 July 2025 or after 30 June 2027 are refused. The calculator follows the ATO’s view of ordinary time earnings. In Department of Education v Commissioner of Taxation [2026] FCA 898 the Federal Court held that a salary loading allowance paid on top of the salary for ordinary hours was not ordinary time earnings; the Commissioner has appealed, will not finalise the draft qualifying earnings ruling until the appeal ends, and meanwhile decides by the existing view. The outcome may change how loadings are treated. Results are planning estimates: check them against the award or agreement and the ATO’s guidance.

## How the contractor against employee calculator works

The calculator takes one person doing the same work for the same hours for a full income year, as an employee and as a contractor. The employee is paid the salary, which includes paid leave. The leave loading is the stated rate on the annual leave pay, a 52nd of the salary for each week of leave. The super guarantee is the year’s rate on the salary and, when it is ordinary time earnings, the loading, up to the year’s maximum contributions base, which before 2026-27 applied each quarter: the page assumes even quarters. Payroll tax and the workers compensation premium are the rates you state on the salary and loading, with the super guarantee when you say it counts.

The contractor is paid the hourly rate for the hours the employee would work: the hours a week times the 52 weeks of the year less the annual and other paid leave. The business pays GST on top when the contractor is registered, and its cost includes that GST only when it cannot claim it back. Under section 12(3) of the Superannuation Guarantee (Administration) Act 1992, a person who works under a contract that is wholly or principally for their labour is an employee of the other party for the super guarantee, even with an ABN. TR 2023/4 says the contract must be with the person as an individual, give no right to delegate, subcontract or assign the work, not be for a result and be mainly for labour. When all 4 hold on your answers, the business owes the super guarantee on the labour part of the fee, which section 10A(1)(d) makes qualifying earnings. Payroll tax and workers compensation apply to the labour part only when you say the state’s law reaches the contractor, a simplification: each scheme can value the amount differently.

The take-home figures are for an Australian resident with no other income, using this site’s income tax engines. The employee’s salary and loading take income tax, the Medicare levy and any surcharge or study loan repayment, after the $1,000 standard deduction from 2026-27. The contractor’s fee less business expenses and their own deductible super is taxed the same way as a sole trader’s profit, with the small business income tax offset when you say it applies. Each amount is worked exactly and rounded to the cent once.

## Employee or contractor: what decides it

The calculator does not decide whether the person is an employee: it works both answers from your facts. Whether a person is an employee in the ordinary meaning depends on the whole relationship between the parties. Section 15AA of the Fair Work Act 2009 and the ATO’s TR 2023/4 set out the tests, and the ATO’s Employee or independent contractor pages include its decision tool. A contractor who is an employee in the ordinary meaning is owed the super guarantee on all of their ordinary time earnings, whatever section 12(3) says. Payroll tax and workers compensation each have their own tests in each state and territory. For income tax, see the personal services income checker: the PSI rules can deny a contractor deductions and the small business income tax offset.

## What the contractor against employee calculator does not cover

The calculator does not decide whether the person is an employee, whether section 12(3) applies when labour is the largest part of the fee without being more than half, or whether a contractor is in payroll tax or workers compensation: you answer those. It holds no award or agreement rates: the leave, loading, payroll tax rate and workers compensation rate are what you enter. It does not work payroll tax thresholds, grouping or deductions (see the payroll tax calculator), workers compensation by industry (see the workers compensation calculator), overtime, penalty rates, allowances, long service leave, notice or redundancy pay, or leave paid out when employment ends. It leaves out the PSI rules, Division 293 tax, excess concessional contributions, salary sacrifice, contributions tax in the fund, PAYG withholding and instalments, other income, part years, non-residents, contracts through a company or trust, labour hire and domestic or private work of 30 hours a week or less (section 12(11)). ## How the 13-week cash forecast works

The 13-week cash forecast works out closing cash for each week and the funding gap against a buffer you set. Weekly closing cash = opening cash + receipts − payments. Each closing balance opens the next week. Funding gap = minimum buffer − lowest opening or weekly closing cash, with a floor of zero.

No statutory rate or threshold is applied. The layout follows business.gov.au: cash flow.

## What the 13-week cash forecast does not decide

The funding gap is a planning gap, not a lending decision. The 13-week cash forecast does not predict receipts or payments, and weekly totals can hide a shortage within the week. A receipt delayed beyond week 13 is reported separately and does not enter the closing balance.

## How the objection deadline checker works

The objection deadline checker gives the last day to lodge a taxation objection under section 14ZW of the Taxation Administration Act 1953. An income tax assessment allows 2 years after notice of the assessment is given when item 1, 2, 3 or 3A of the table in subsection 170(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 applies, and 4 years otherwise. An FBT assessment allows 4 years. Any other decision for which the section sets no other period allows 60 days after notice is served.

An amended assessment allows whichever ends later: 60 days after notice of the amended assessment, or the 2 or 4 years after notice of the assessment it amends. A 2-year period runs from that notice even where the assessment amended was itself an amended one; a 4-year period runs from notice of the first assessment for that year. The checker shows both dates and the later one.

Each period starts the day after notice, because subsection 36(1) of the Acts Interpretation Act 1901 leaves out the day a period begins after. A 2-year period from notice given on 28 February 2026 therefore starts on 1 March 2026 and ends on 29 February 2028. When the last day is a Saturday or Sunday, subsection 36(2) lets the objection be lodged on the next day that is not a weekend or holiday. The Act’s own example: a person who has until Saturday 31 March may apply on Monday 2 April.

## What the objection deadline checker does not cover

The checker does not decide whether a decision can be objected to, which item of subsection 170(1) applies, or when notice was given, so keep the notice’s issue and receipt dates. It does not know public holidays: they differ by state and town, so check the last day and the day after it where you lodge. It does not cover private rulings, administrative penalties, retained refunds, deceased estates, specialised taxes or the other decisions for which section 14ZW sets its own period, or the time to seek review of an objection decision. Confirm the date with the notice of decision or a registered tax agent.

## How the PAYG instalments calculator works

The PAYG instalments calculator works out one quarterly pay as you go instalment by the method on the activity statement. Under option 1 the instalment is the amount the ATO notifies at T7. Varying it, the first instalment of the income year is 25% of the estimated tax for the year, the second 50%, the third 75% and the fourth 100%, each less the earlier instalments. The third and fourth add back any credits you were entitled to claim for earlier instalments, claimed or not; no credit can arise before the second, so the calculator refuses one entered for the first or second. A negative result is a credit to claim at 5B.

Under option 2 the instalment is the quarter’s instalment income at T1 times the instalment rate at T2. A varied rate is the estimated tax for the year divided by the estimated instalment income, times 100. If the varied rate is lower than the rate used for the previous quarter, the earlier instalments less any credits claimed, less the earlier instalment income times the varied rate, is a credit for 5B. That comparison is with last quarter’s rate, not a new rate the ATO gives at T2. No step is rounded: only the instalment and the credit drop their cents, so 25% of an estimate of $399.99 is $99.9975, an instalment of $99.

For 2026-27, the instalment amount the ATO notifies already includes its GDP adjustment of 5%, which raises the base year’s income to allow for growth; the adjustment does not change an instalment rate. The ATO’s own example: instalment income of $20,100 at a rate of 1.7% gives $341.70, entered as 341 at T11 and 5A.

## What the PAYG instalments calculator does not cover

The calculator does not work out instalment income, the tax on it or whether you can choose between the 2 options. It does not cover monthly instalments, the 2 instalments a year some payers make, annual instalments or consolidated groups, and it does not estimate general interest charge on a variation that proves too low. It does not check whether you may claim a credit or when an instalment is due. Confirm the figures with the ATO’s PAYG instalments calculator or a registered tax agent.

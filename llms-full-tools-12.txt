## How the CGT calculator works

The CGT calculator works out the capital gain or capital loss from one sale (CGT event A1) for an Australian resident individual, trust, complying super fund or company, for a sale contract from 1 July 2020 to 30 June 2027. The sale contract’s date decides the income year and the 12 months held; the cost base adds the purchase price, buying costs, capital improvements, title costs, selling costs and, for an asset bought after 20 August 1991, costs of owning that were not deducted, less capital works deductions.

It then works the gain by each method the owner can use. The discount method halves the gain of an individual or trust, and takes a third off a complying super fund’s, once the asset was held at least 12 months, not counting the day bought or the day sold. The indexation method, for an asset bought before 21 September 1999 (sections 114-1 and 114-10 end it at 11.45 am that day, so a purchase on the day is refused), indexes each cost (except costs of owning) to the September 1999 quarter; a company must use it and gets no discount. Capital losses this year and then net capital losses from earlier years come off the gain before the discount, which is why indexation sometimes wins.

For a home, it counts the days of the ownership period, from settlement to settlement, when the home was not the main residence: before moving in, unless that was as soon as practicable, and each period away that is not treated as the main residence. A period away can be treated as the main residence indefinitely while empty, and for 6 years while rented, the 6 years starting again each time you move back in. The taxable share is those days over the ownership period, plus the floor area used to produce income for the days it was. If the home was first rented or used to produce income after 20 August 1996, was fully exempt until then and is now only partly exempt, section 118-192 treats it as bought then for its market value, and the method follows that date.

The result gives the amounts for question 18 of the supplementary tax return, in whole dollars as the return takes them, and for an individual an estimate of the extra tax and Medicare levy.

## How to enter a sale in the CGT calculator

Enter the dates of the contracts, not of settlement: the sale contract’s date is when the CGT event happens (subsection 104-10(3)). Put stamp duty and legal fees on buying in buying costs, with the date they were incurred; a deposit paid at the contract is part of the purchase price. Put capital improvements, and costs of defending your title, in the 2 cost rows with their dates; add 2 costs incurred in the same quarter together. Selling costs are agent’s commission, advertising and legal fees on the sale. Costs of owning are interest, rates, land tax, insurance and repairs you could not deduct, such as for a home or vacant land. Leave out any cost to the extent you deducted or can deduct it (subsections 110-45(1B) and (2)), except capital works: enter those costs in full and the deductions in their own field. Leave out depreciating assets such as carpets and appliances, which have their own balancing adjustment.

For a home, enter the settlement dates and the day you moved in, then each period you lived elsewhere before the sale. Tick ‘Rented’ for a period it was rented or available for rent, and ‘Choose’ for the periods you treat as your main residence; you cannot treat another home as your main residence for those periods (subsection 118-145(4)). A rented room or a surgery goes in the floor area fields, with the days that use ran; use that continued while you were away stays taxable (subsection 118-190(3)). Enter the market value at first use to produce income in case section 118-192 applies; the result says whether it did.

## What the CGT calculator does not cover

The calculator gives an estimate for one sale, and no advice. It does not cover:

- sale contracts from 1 July 2027, under Schedule 1 to the Treasury Laws Amendment (Tax Reform No. 1) Act 2026, and before 1 July 2020;
- foreign and temporary residents, life insurance companies, collectables, personal use assets, and CGT events other than a sale;
- the test of section 115-45 for shares or trust interests held at least 10% with associates, a rented absence that ends on the day the ATO’s examples 72 and 80 count the 6 years apart, use of part of a home to produce income that began while you were away (subsection 118-190(3)), and costs of owning a home when section 118-192 applies;
- other capital gains in the same year, and splitting a parcel of shares between the methods, as example 12 of the ATO’s guide does;
- the small business concessions in Division 152, roll-overs, and the extra discount for affordable housing (section 115-125);
- the 6 months for 2 homes when changing main residences (section 118-140), land over 2 hectares, spouses with different main residences, inherited homes and building on land you owned;
- indexing capital works deductions, which come off at their face amount, capital works deductions on an asset bought before 13 May 1997 and improved after 30 June 1999 (subsection 110-45(1A)), and a substituted accounting period.

Confirm the figure with the ATO or a registered tax agent before relying on it.

## One worked example

Take $1,100 that is assumed to be wholly taxable and GST-inclusive at 10%. Enter this formula in a cell of the Ozzit workbook:

```
=oz.GSTExtractλ(1100)
```

The result is 100.00. Check: 1100 / 11 = 100.00.

That is the whole of the function’s contribution: 10% arithmetic on an amount you supply.

Whether the supply is wholly taxable, whether a tax invoice supports it and whether the credit is claimable in that period are decisions for the reviewer. The function cannot see the evidence and does not classify the supply.

The 15 November 2026 native Excel record evaluates this formula against the exact v3.6.0 release workbook. It returned 99.999999999999986 before display rounding. The file hash was unchanged after the check. The repository also retains its historical v3.2.0 screenshot record.

## Limits

The functions calculate; a person decides the accounting treatment and signs off.

- The GST helpers apply 10% unless you supply another rate. They do not classify a supply, test registration or prepare a BAS.
- The ATO benchmark ratio helpers restate the ATO’s arithmetic for amounts you supply. They ship no benchmark ranges and do not read a tax return.
- oz.PrimeCostλ and oz.DiminishingValueλ are modelling schedules. They are not ATO Division 40 methods and they do not implement immediate write-off or low-value pools.
- The AASB 16 functions are lessee arithmetic. They do not decide the lease term, what counts as a lease payment or whether an arrangement contains a lease, and they do not cover lessor accounting or the short-term and low-value exemptions.
- The library contains no individual income tax, Medicare levy, Division 7A interest or tax rate cards.
- The 13-week cash flow, practice calculator and retention reconciliation workbooks in the repository’s templates folder are separate templates, not functions in this library.
- Known defects are listed in the repository’s changelog and issues. Check a schedule against an independent calculation before relying on it.

## Common questions

### What is Ozzit?

An Excel workbook containing 150 native LAMBDA functions and 5 help tables for financial modelling under Australian conventions, published free under the Ryan Duguid Public Attribution Licence 1.0. It has no add-in, macro or external dependency.

### Which Excel versions can run it?

Microsoft 365 or Excel 2024 or later, which support LAMBDA and dynamic arrays. LibreOffice and earlier Excel versions cannot evaluate the functions.

### Does Ozzit calculate Australian tax?

No. It performs 10% GST arithmetic and labels Australian financial years, and its depreciation and lease functions are modelling schedules rather than ATO or standard-setting methods. It contains no income tax, Medicare levy or Division 7A calculations.

### Where do I get the current release?

Release v3.7.0, published on 18 November 2026, is the current published release; this page documents v3.6.0, published on 14 November 2026. v3.7.0 adds a PAYG withholding function, which returns the amount to withhold from a weekly, fortnightly, monthly or quarterly payment under the ATO’s Schedule 1 formulas from a coefficient table you supply, bringing the total to 151, and fixes 3 known issues in v3.6.0. Read the v3.7.0 release page. v3.6.0 adds 7 functions, bringing the total to 150, and is the first release under the Ryan Duguid Public Attribution Licence 1.0; v3.5.0 stays under the Apache License 2.0. Each release ships ozzit.xlsx with a SHA256SUMS file and a provenance record on its GitHub release page. Compare the downloaded file’s SHA-256 with the published value before use. Each named formula is a native Excel LAMBDA in the free Ozzit workbook, or one of its 5 help tables. It needs Microsoft 365 or Excel 2024 or later. Enter a call in a cell of the workbook, or copy the definition from its name manager into your own model. Leave room for any spilled result.

### oz.IsOccurrenceDateλ

oz.IsOccurrenceDateλ(Dates, FirstOccurrence, [LastOccurrence], [Repeats])

Returns TRUE if Date passed is when something happens. This was developed for budgeting where we have expenses that start, and sometimes repeat at regular intervals.

Returns TRUE if a Date is when something happens. Developed for budgeting, where expenses start and sometimes repeat at regular intervals.

Note: A monthly, quarterly, semi-annual or annual item that starts on the 29th, 30th or 31st falls on the last day of any shorter month.

Parameters:

Dates (required)

Date to test. Normally this is horizontal timeline by day

FirstOccurrence (required)

When something that may repeat, happens for the first time.

LastOccurrence (optional)

When something that may repeat, happens for the last time. If omitted, it is assumed repeating items repeat forever.

Repeats (optional)

A code that conveys how often this thing repeats. Repeat codes are O=One time/never repeats (default) A=Annually S=Semi-Annually Q=Quarterly M=Monthly B=BiWeekly W=Weekly X=Every Workday (Monday to Friday) D=Daily

```
=SUM(N(oz.IsOccurrenceDateλ(SEQUENCE(,90,"1/7/2026"), "1/7/2026", "10/9/2026", "D")))
```

### oz.RateAtDateλ

oz.RateAtDateλ(EffectiveDates, Rates, Dates)

Returns the rate in effect on each date from a table of effective dates and rates, refusing gaps, unsorted or repeated dates

Returns the rate in effect on each date from your table of effective dates and rates.

Note: Each rate applies from its effective date until the next one. Dates may be one date or a row or column of them, and the result takes their shape. A date before the first effective date has no rate in effect and returns #N/A. The table must be complete and in order, so instead of a result it returns a message when EffectiveDates and Rates differ in length or hold a blank or text entry, when the dates are not in ascending order, or when a date repeats. Parameters:

EffectiveDates (required)

A row or column of dates from which each rate applies, in ascending order.

Rates (required)

The rate that applies from each effective date, the same length as EffectiveDates.

Dates (required)

The date, or a row or column of dates, to look up.

```

```

### oz.DateDifλ

oz.DateDifλ(StartDate, EndDate, [Unit])

Whole years, months or days between two dates, and the remainders DATEDIF gets wrong

Whole years, months or days between two dates, and the remainders.

Note: Excel documents DATEDIF as a legacy compatibility function and warns its MD unit can return a negative or wrong day count. This counts a month as complete when EDATE() of the start date has arrived, so a month after 31 January is the last day of February, and takes every remainder from there. A reversed range or an unknown unit returns #NUM!.

Parameters:

StartDate (required)

The earlier date, numeric or text.

EndDate (required)

The later date, numeric or text.

Unit (optional, Default = D)

Y whole years, M whole months, D days, YM months left after the whole years, YD days left after the whole years, MD days left after the whole months.

```
=oz.DateDifλ(DATE(2026,1,31), DATE(2026,3,1), "MD")
```

## How the vehicle classifier works

The vehicle classifier works out whether one vehicle bought in 2025-26 or 2026-27 is a car for income tax, GST and FBT, and what follows. The designed load is the gross vehicle mass on the compliance plate less the basic kerb weight, or the stated payload, which must agree with them. Under section 995-1 of ITAA 1997, a motor vehicle other than a motor cycle is a car if designed to carry a load under 1 tonne and fewer than 9 passengers, the driver counted.

For a car, the classifier asks whether it was designed principally to carry passengers. A passenger body was. A dual cab was if its seats, at 68 kg each (MT 2024), weigh more than the load left. For a single cab ute or panel van the answer turns on factors the page does not weigh (TD 94/19). The dual cab test is MT 2024’s, for FBT; no ATO ruling applies it to the car limit. So for a passenger body section 40-230 caps the cost to depreciate at the car limit, after any GST credit, and for a dual cab, a single cab ute or a panel van the car limit is not decided here; the page still gives a cost after the credit that is within the limit, as the limit would not reduce it. Section 69-10 of the GST Act caps the credit at 1/11 of the limit.

## Luxury car tax, the GST credit and FBT

Luxury car tax has its own car: a load under 2 tonnes and fewer than 9 passengers. The tax can apply above the threshold for its fuel consumption, unless it is a commercial vehicle not designed principally to carry passengers. For utes the classifier uses LCTD 2023/1’s simplified method, the ATO’s compliance approach: seats at 68 kg weighing less than half the load at supply, which is the designed load less any accessories then fitted. That approach says when the ATO will not review a sale, not whether the vehicle is a commercial vehicle in law, so it does not lift the GST credit cap: above the car limit the credit on a ute or van that is a car is not decided here. At exactly half, the 2 tests part: MT 2024 counts a dual cab as designed for passengers only when its seats weigh more than the load left, whilst LCTD 2023/1’s method needs them to weigh less than half the load. A 5-seat dual cab with a 680 kg load can therefore be exempt from FBT, whilst luxury car tax and the GST credit cap are not decided here.

For FBT, a single cab ute, a panel van, a dual cab not designed principally for passengers and any vehicle that is not a car can be exempt only if an employee’s private use was work-related travel or minor, infrequent and irregular use. You state that fact; the classifier never concludes it. A car designed principally for passengers gives a car fringe benefit, which the FBT car calculator values.

TD 94/19’s 4-wheel drive wagon, with 5 seats and an 865 kg load, carries passengers; at $75,000 in 2025-26 the ATO’s instant asset write-off page gives a $6,334 credit and a $68,666 cost, or the $69,674 car limit without a credit. MT 2024’s dual cab has 340 kg of passengers against 260 kg left. LCTD 2023/1’s example 5, a dual cab with 5 seats and 960 kg, is one the ATO will not review for luxury car tax. TD 2006/40’s $5,182 credit is $5,182.64 here, with its cents.

## What the vehicle classifier does not cover

The classifier does not decide whether private use was minor, infrequent and irregular, or whether a dual cab, single cab ute or panel van was designed mainly for carrying passengers for the car limit. Nor does it decide whether a ute or van is a commercial vehicle for the GST credit cap, whether a modification permanently changed a vehicle’s design (MT 2033 and LCTD 2023/1 paragraphs 16 to 30), or the principal purpose of a truck, bus or other body under 1 tonne, which turns on factors it does not weigh. Where LCTD 2023/1’s simplified method is not met it says the question is not decided here, and that method assumes a ute the Australian Design Rules class as a goods vehicle. It does not cover cars fitted out to carry people in wheelchairs, emergency vehicles, motor homes and campervans, taxis and limousines, leases and hire, a dealer quoting an ABN, a partly creditable purchase, or a year before 2025-26. It does not work out decline in value, luxury car tax payable or the taxable value of a fringe benefit. A classification is not advice: confirm it with the ATO’s guidance or a registered tax agent.

## How the PCG 2021/4 risk scorer works

Practical Compliance Guideline PCG 2021/4 sets out how the ATO rates the risk that an individual professional practitioner (IPP) does not return enough of their professional firm’s profit in their own hands. The scorer first asks the 2 gateways. If the arrangement is not commercially driven (Gateway 1) or has a high-risk feature (Gateway 2), it gives no score, because paragraph 12 makes the risk assessment framework unavailable.

It then scores up to 3 factors from 1 (lowest risk) to 6 (highest), using Table 1 at paragraph 76: the share of the whole of firm group profit entitlement the IPP returns personally, the total effective tax rate paid by the IPP and associated entities on that income, and, if you choose, the IPP’s remuneration as a percentage of a commercial benchmark for their services. It adds the scores and places the total in the green, amber or red zone of Table 2 at paragraph 78. If the IPP returns the whole entitlement, paragraph 81 makes the arrangement low risk and no other factor is scored.

The scorer reproduces the scores and zones of all 12 case studies in the guideline’s appendix. Case study 1 opens the page: Nicolas returns $330,000 of a $600,000 entitlement, 55% and a score of 4, and he and his company pay $189,367 of tax on it, 31.56% and a score of 3, so his aggregate of 7 is green. In case study 7, Jacinta’s $360,000 against a $350,000 benchmark adds a factor 3 score of 3, for an aggregate of 10 against all 3 factors, also green.

## What the PCG 2021/4 risk scorer does not cover

The scorer does not decide whether the guideline applies: whether the IPP provides professional services or manages the firm and is an equity holder, whether the firm’s income is personal services income and whether the structure is legally effective (paragraph 28). It does not test the gateways; it takes your answers. It does not work out the entitlement, the tax for each entity under paragraph 85, a part-time IPP’s full-time equivalent (paragraph 71) or a commercial benchmark; the PCG 2021/4 allocation calculator works out each recipient’s tax. A green zone is not a ruling: PCG 2021/4 does not change the law, Part IVA can still apply, and the ATO may still review an arrangement whose profit allocation drifts (paragraph 107). Confirm the assessment with the guideline or a registered tax agent.

## How the PCG 2021/4 allocation calculator works

The PCG 2021/4 allocation calculator works out the tax each recipient of a professional firm’s profit pays on it, then scores the 3 risk assessment factors from those figures. It takes the IPP and up to 5 associated entities, each with its firm income, firm-related deductions and other income.

For an individual it applies the year’s resident scale, and for a company the year’s company rate. Paragraph 85 counts the larger of the tax on the firm income alone and the extra tax the firm income adds to the recipient’s other income. In example 4 of the guideline, an associate with $100,000 of firm income and $50,000 of other income pays $40,567 on both and would pay $6,717 without the firm income, so $33,850 counts, more than the $22,967 on the firm income alone.

Factor 1 is the IPP’s share of the total firm income, factor 2 the tax counted over that income, and factor 3, if you choose it, the IPP’s firm income against your benchmark. The scores and zone then follow Tables 1 and 2. The calculator reproduces the effective tax rate and scores of the guideline’s case studies 1 to 12 and example 4, and the tax in examples 5 and 6. For Bernie in case study 4 and Frank in case study 5 the resident scale gives $1 more tax than the guideline prints; the rates and scores are the same.

## What the PCG 2021/4 allocation calculator does not cover

The PCG 2021/4 allocation calculator does not tax a non-resident, a person under 18 on trust income (Division 6AA), a trustee taxed under section 98 or 99A, a superannuation fund or fringe benefits: it refuses them. Paragraphs 72, 73 and 95 to 99 count superannuation contributions and FBT in the effective tax rate but do not say how they count in factor 1, so work those out yourself and use the risk scorer. It applies no tax offset, such as the low income tax offset or a franking tax offset, because the guideline’s examples apply none; where your tax paid differs, use the risk scorer with your own figure.

It offers 2022-23 to 2026-27, the years since the guideline applied from 1 July 2022 (paragraph 17), and 2020-21 for the guideline’s examples. It does not test the gateways or decide whether the guideline applies (paragraph 28), and it takes each figure as you enter it, with no full-time equivalent for a part-time IPP (paragraph 71) and no commercial benchmark of its own. A green zone is not a ruling: Part IVA can still apply (paragraphs 36 and 37). Confirm the assessment with the guideline or a registered tax agent.

## How the GIC payment plan calculator works

The calculator compares paying a tax debt in full today with paying it by equal instalments. It works day by day from the first instalment to the last. Each day, the unpaid debt and any unpaid GIC grow by that day’s rate: the quarter’s annual GIC rate divided by 365, or 366 in a leap year, as sections 8AAC and 8AAD of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 set out. It finds the equal instalment that clears the debt and its GIC on the last instalment day, rounds it down to the cent, and lets the final instalment pay what is left. The GIC is the total paid on the plan less the debt.

In the example the page opens with, a $1,000 debt is paid in 3 monthly instalments from 1 October 2026, all at the October to December 2026 rate of 11.51%. Paying $336.56 on 1 October leaves $663.44, which grows by 31 days of GIC to $669.96 by 1 November. Paying $336.56 then leaves $333.40, which grows by 30 days to $336.56 on 1 December, the final instalment. The plan costs $1,009.68, so $9.68 of GIC over paying now.

## What the GIC payment plan calculator does not cover

The calculator does not work out the debt, its due date or any GIC that accrued before the plan starts; enter the debt as it stands on the first instalment day. It does not model a missed or late instalment, a plan the ATO varies, a GIC remission, a refund offset against the debt or the deductibility of GIC. Rates after December 2026 are your assumption until the ATO publishes them. The ATO sets the instalments of an actual plan, and its figures may differ by cents from these. ## How the ATO penalties and interest calculator works

The calculator works one question at a time, with the same rules, rounding and refusals as the ato-interest-penalties command in the australian-accounting calculators. GIC compounds daily from the due day on the unpaid amount and unpaid GIC, at each quarter’s annual rate divided by 365, or 366 in a leap year (sections 8AAC and 8AAD). A payment reduces the balance before that day’s charge. SIC compounds the same way on the additional tax, at the GIC rate less 4 points, from the day the first assessment’s tax was due to the day before the amended assessment’s notice (sections 280-100 and 280-105). Both are worked exactly and rounded to the cent once.

The failure to lodge penalty is 1 penalty unit for each 28 days or part of 28 days late, at most 5, each at the unit applying when its period starts: $330 to 30 June 2026 and $364 from 1 July 2026. It is doubled for a medium entity, times 5 for a large entity and times 500 for a significant global entity (section 286-80). The statement penalty is 75%, 50% or 25% of the shortfall for intentional disregard, recklessness or a failure to take reasonable care, or 60, 40 or 20 penalty units with no shortfall. A position not reasonably arguable gives 25% where its shortfall is more than the greater of $10,000 and 1% of the return’s income tax. The base amount is increased 20% where section 284-220 applies and reduced 80%, to nil or 20% for a voluntary disclosure (section 284-225).

The ATO pays simple interest at the base interest rate, the GIC rate less 7 points, on a payment of income tax, SIC, a compulsory study loan repayment or an FS assessment debt made more than 14 days early, to the end of the due day (sections 8A to 8C), and on relevant tax overpaid because of a decision and then refunded, from the later of the notice and the payment (sections 9 and 10). The interest rounds to the cent, and under 50 cents is not paid.

In the example the page opens with, $10,000 due on 21 November 2025 and unpaid to 30 June 2026 grows by 222 days of GIC at 10.61%, 10.65% and 10.96% to $10,676.86: $676.86 of GIC.

## What the ATO penalties and interest calculator does not cover

It does not work scheme penalties, the trust and partnership items of section 284-90, Division 296 GIC, a payment refunded before its due day or interest on a running balance account surplus. It refuses days without a published rate: GIC after 31 December 2026 unless you assume a rate, which makes the result an estimate, and SIC and credit interest after that day. The October to December 2026 base interest rate of 4.51% is the published GIC rate less 7 points; the ATO’s credit interest page lists quarters only to September 2026. The behaviour behind a shortfall, whether a position is reasonably arguable, whether a disclosure was voluntary and the entity’s size are your facts; the calculator does not decide them. ## How the Division 296 tax calculator works

The Division 296 tax calculator works out one person’s Division 296 tax for 2026-27, the first year it applies. ## How the Division 7A loan schedule calculator works

The Division 7A loan schedule calculator sets out a complying loan from a private company year by year, from the first income year after the year it was made to the end of its term. Each year it works the minimum yearly repayment under subsection 109E(6) at that year’s benchmark rate, the interest at that rate, how much of the repayment reduces the loan and the balance left at 30 June. Leave the repayments blank to pay the minimum each year; enter more in any year to see the later minimums fall and the loan end sooner.

In the last year the minimum is the balance and a full year’s interest. A repayment on 30 June carries that day on the reduced balance, so repaying the loan then needs a day’s interest less than the minimum; a blank entry repays the loan and no more, and a repayment that repays the loan is no shortfall.

If a year’s repayment is less than its minimum, subsection 109E(1) takes the company to pay a dividend of the shortfall at the end of that year, limited by section 109Y to its distributable surplus. The schedule stops at that year. The heading of subsection 109E(1) speaks of the first year in which a payment falls short, and the sources applied do not say how later years are worked.

The minimum for a single year matches the Division 7A minimum yearly repayment calculator. The ATO’s worked example on Loans by private companies takes $55,000 at 5.95% over 7 years for $9,835 and then $50,430 at 5.45% over 6 years for $10,079, to the nearest dollar; those years are before the published rates this page holds.

## What the Division 7A loan schedule calculator does not cover

The calculator does not decide whether a payment is a loan, whether the agreement is in writing before the lodgment day or charges at least the benchmark rate, or whether a mortgage meets the 110% test: it takes the term you choose. It works only a loan whose written term is the maximum, 7 or 25 years: subsection 109E(6) counts the remaining term from the loan’s own term, so a loan agreed for fewer years has higher minimums than this page shows. It refuses a loan made before 4 December 1997, which Division 7A reaches only once its term or amount is varied (subsection 109D(5)); a loan made before 1 July 2020, whose first year is before the ATO rates it holds; a loan made through an interposed entity (Subdivision E); a loan that refinances another or was extended under a mortgage, whose term subsections 109N(3A) to (3D) and 109E(3A) and (3B) reduce or restart; and a repayment made between 30 June and the lodgment day for the year the loan was made, which the ATO takes off the loan and also counts in the next year.

It assumes each repayment is made on 30 June and that the company’s income year ends on 30 June. A repayment made earlier in a year carries less interest. It does not apply section 109Q, which lets the Commissioner disregard a shortfall that would cause undue hardship, section 109R, which disregards a repayment funded by a new loan from the company, section 109Y’s limit or the regulations that subsection 109E(5) allows. It does not combine loans with different terms: each is a separate amalgamated loan with its own schedule.

The funding dividend is arithmetic on the marginal rate you give. It does not check that the company has the profits or franking credits to pay it, and it is not advice on whether to repay by dividend, salary or cash. Future benchmark rates are not known: the figures from 2027-28 rest on your assumption. Confirm the figures with the ATO Division 7A calculator and decision tool or a registered tax agent.

## How the CGT concessions checker works

The CGT concessions checker takes one capital gain from selling an asset used in a business and works through Division 152 in the order the Act sets. It first tests the basic conditions in subsection 152-10(1): at least one of a CGT small business entity, the maximum net asset value test or a passively held asset, and the active asset test. Each of the 4 concessions then has its own conditions, and the result says, for each, whether it is open and which provision decides it.

A CGT small business entity carries on a business, or is winding one up, and has an aggregated turnover under $2 million: for the previous year, as likely for the year of the sale at its first day or the day the business started (unless both of the 2 years before were $2 million or more), or for the year of the sale at its end. For a partnership asset, the partnership is tested, not the partner. The net asset value test counts the market value of the CGT assets of you, your affiliates and the entities connected with you, less the liabilities and provisions related to them; an individual leaves out personal use assets, the main residence (except the share subsection 152-20(2A) counts for its use to produce income), super and life insurance (section 152-20).

The active asset test counts the days from the purchase contract to the sale contract, or to an earlier end of the business when the sale came within 12 months of it, both days included. An asset owned 15 years or less must have been active for at least half of those days; one owned more than 15 years, for at least 7.5 years. Because 7.5 years is 2,736 to 2,746 days, depending on the leap days and months the periods span, a total from 2,736 to 2,745 is refused: count it in years and months.

The 15-year exemption needs 15 years of continuous ownership before the sale and an individual aged 55 or over who is retiring, or one permanently incapacitated; for a company or trust, the individual is its significant individual, and it must have had one for a total of 15 years. When it applies, the whole gain is disregarded and the other concessions are not needed. Otherwise the 50% reduction applies automatically, and the retirement exemption and roll-over can follow in either order.

## What the CGT concessions checker does not cover

The checker says which concessions are open, and gives no advice. It does not work out the capital gain or the amount each concession takes off it. It does not cover:

- sale contracts from 1 July 2027, when Schedule 1 to the Treasury Laws Amendment (Tax Reform No. 1) Act 2026 changes Division 152, and before 1 July 2020;
- a share in a company or an interest in a trust, which must also meet the look-through conditions in subsection 152-10(2);
- a depreciating asset, whose gain is disregarded (section 118-24) or comes from CGT event K7, which section 152-10(1) excludes;
- CGT events other than a sale, including CGT events D1, J2, J5 and J6, and rights to partnership income or capital (subsection 152-10(2C));
- working out aggregated turnover, net value, affiliates, connected entities and small business participation percentages, which it takes from you;
- involuntary disposals and relationship breakdown roll-overs, which can extend the ownership and active periods (sections 152-45 and 152-115);
- public entities, which cannot choose the retirement exemption (subsection 152-305(3)), look-through earnout rights, and substituted accounting periods.
